Three things happened last week.

A friend encouraged me to take melatonin, or magnesium glycinate for
fixing my sleep schedule. To me, it sounded like a recreational drug- "vivid,
intense, or weird dreams". This is exactly the sort of recreational drug that I
_might_ touch. (This, and laughing gas. No further). On cue, I pulled out the
["Good Sleep, Good Learning, Good
Life"](https://super-memory.com/articles/sleep.htm) post from Piotr Wozniak.
The friened pointed out that I have a tick- I'll remember specific blog posts
or videos about the topic topic being discussed in a conversation.

I met an old friend, and I was trying to explain why, atleast with people that
you trust, you should be completely open about your whole thought process.
(Another friend claims that I do this to get "partial marks" for showing
my work). So I forced her to read Devi Parikh's post ["You should tell them
that"](https://deviparikh.substack.com/p/you-should-tell-them-that). The same
friend was looking for things to read that would make her _think_ about
different aspects of life.

Finally, I went down a Claude rabbit hole looking for good resources on
physiology, metabolism, and such. I wanted a _dense_, _scientific_
understanding of how exercise, diet, sleep and stress affect health (immunity,
capability, mental health). I ended up finding [Why Zebras Don't Get
Ulcers](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D9H9qTdserM) by Robert Sapolsky. I
shared the link, and a friend loved it. I then dumped links to _all_ the videos
/ lectures that have affected my thinking (and hopefully) my behavior, over the
years.

A digression: I think its a pity that we don't canonical references on holistic
health. Something in the spirit of "The Selfish Gene", or "Operating Systems:
Three Easy Pieces"- dense but readable text, that builds up an argument, and
gives you a mental model of how the thing works. My lifestyle, more so than
anything is going to help me prevent the degradation of my body- yet there is
no "Doctor of Lifestyle", and there is no canonical reference on how to do
these things well. Is it really as simple as regular sleep times, eating
less-processed, nutritious foods, strength training, regular movement, and (it
feels stupid to say this) coping with stress properly? Or is there nuance?  How
do I understand the nuance? I didn't find Outlive satisfying yet. I _will_ read
Guyton's Physiology if it comes down to it, but I won't find that satisfying
either.


It has been a while since I declared "[nonfiction is for impressing people in
conversations](https://akshayrdeodhar.github.io/2023/04/26/nonfiction.html)".
My opinions have since changed. 

For one, nonfiction is _fun_. A well written argument, like reading a math
proof has "aha" moments, and points where you are banging your head against a
wall. With the caveat that I only read things that I was curious about,
nonfiction did change my model of the world, which eventually seeped into my
behavior.

There are also some lectures- videos or talks, mostly from academicians, that
have had the same effect. The nice thing is, it is really easy to rewatch a
lecture, than it is to reread a book. Some of these

This article is meant as a listicle of these talks, and why I think they are
interesting, useful, and/or amusing.


- [Marcus Aurelius' Meditations: The Stoic Ideal, Michael Surgue](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Auuk1y4DRgk)
    - A charismatic (good looking) philosophy professor pacing around in the
      classroom, _passionately_ talking about "Meditations"- the musings of the
      Stoic Roman emperor, Marcus Aurelius. _There's things that you can control, and
      things that you cannot control. If you can't control it, then there's no point
      whining about it. If you can control it- your intentions, your behavior, who do
      you expect to help you out but yourself? Marcus accepts no complaints and no
      excuses, because there are no excuses to give!_. I have turned to this
      in times that require virtue and strength. Much of the charm comes from the way Surgue speaks,
      getting excited, his voice rising, as he fires off one idea after another. Also, really cool title
      music. There's something about these old videos- Friedman's "Free to choose", the "Structure and Interpretation of
      Computer Programs" lecture series- all of these have really musical openings.


- [Scott Adams: The Happiness
  Formula](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qHEn5LoINj0)
    - I'll start with a caveat- the formula doesn't work- if there were a
      formula that reliably worked, life would be a solved problem. I don't
      sincerely believe that you can program yourself to be happy. BUT- this video
      brings a lot of known scattered ideas together.  Your mood
      ("happiness") is mostly a function of the levels of Serotonin, Dopamine, and Oxytocin. There are _deterministic
      ways_ to influence the levels of these chemicals. You can influence these with your diet, regular
      exercise, sleep, touch, by cultivating a sense of purpose, avoiding stress, and
      by having a social life. "Simple but not easy". Fix all of these, and you'll be
      OK.

- [How to Get a Job at the Big 4, by Sean Lee](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YJZCUhxNCv8)
    - A buff, funny, normal guy from Waterloo explaning how to _systematically_
      break into big tech. There's some very strong advice in here that helped
      me do interviewing right. Practice _thinking out
      loud_, do mock _pair interviews_. Interviewing is a thing that you can get
      good at, with deliberate practice. You don't need to be a genius. _"My GPA is
      2.3! I have been on academic probation for the last 4 years. People come up to
      me and say that they're jealous- but they don't know right? I did not qualify
      for the co-op program. I took 6 years to complete a 4-year degree"_

- [You and Your Research, Richard Hamming](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a1zDuOPkMSw)
    - Yes, _that_ Hamming. _"If what you are working on is not important, and
      is not likely to lead to important things, why aren you working on it?
      After that, I ate with the engineers"_.  A brutal guide to
      doing high quality work (not just research), based on Hamming's years at Bell Labs. Hamming gives no easy solution-
      there is a cost, and you will have to pay it. But he also tries
      to convince you that it _is_ worth the cost.
      

- [Debt: The First 5000 Years, David Graeber](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CZIINXhGDcs)
    - This one opened me to the possibility that my ideas about how the world works could be
      wrong. Graeber presents alternative viewpoints to capitalism, and conventional morality, and
      what a society should be like. But he does this in a very insidious. As an 
      anthropologist, he showers you with examples of societies that did things differently. The
      talk did not turn me into a full-blown anarchist, but it did help me question the ideas I
      believed in. "_Currency is actually invented to pay soldiers. Soldiers are exactly the sort of people
      that currency is meant for. A heavily armed guy who is just passing through is the person who you are 
      least likely to extend credit to. Taxation is a system to make the economy feed your 
      soldiers- soldiers are paid in currency, and citizens must acquire currency to pay taxes_". I'd 
      also recommend the book- but be very careful: Graeber is a silver-tongued anthropologist who is
      trying very hard to make you believe his point of view.


- [Sugar: The Bitter Truth, Dr. Robert Lustig](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dBnniua6-oM)
    - "_What do we call it, when we take in a compound that is foreign to the body, and only
        your liver can metabolise it, and in the process, generates various problems? We call that
        a poison"_. Explains how sugar (fructose) is metabolized, and plainly states that it is a toxin,
        taking the _same_ metabolic pathways as alcohol (which is also a toxin). You weren't supposed
        to eat refind sugar.


- [वजन कमी करणे - समज आणि गैरसमज, Mahendra Gokhale](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hAEufnxbnmA)
    - This one in Marathi. A very Puneri uncle spitting facts about fitness. Masquarades
      as a weight loss talk, then tells you to _exercise_. Another "simple but not easy" talk. Strength training, diet, sleep
      _will_ make you healthy. If you are Marathi, and your parents refuse to exercise, perhaps this will convince them.
      Explains why a pure calorie deficit is a bad idea, and why you should do strength training _regardless of your age_,
      why "walking" is not exercise, and that it _is_ possible to have a nutritous, protein rich, vegetarian diet.



What _is_ it about these videos? The speakers are charismatic, forceful,
confident to the point of being brash, have a sense of humour. They know what
they are talking about, they've spent a lot of time with the subject.
They do not offer a silver bullet. They're trying to make
your life better. I don't know about causality- perhaps I feel strongly about
these ideas because I've watched these videos multiple times. Perhaps I am
drawn to things that I already agree with, I watch lectures which
confirm my biases.

I find myself rewatching these talks from time to time. It takes a while to
re-read a book, but re-watching a lecture is quick and easy. In bad
times, these jolt me out of my spirals, and make me take _one step_.  And in
times when things are going well, I have had some success adopting some of these
ideas in the real world. 

There's a body of experiences, failures and successes and conversations,
people, and writings, and talks that form the "canon" of my identity- what has
value? how do I attain the things that have value? I offer to you a slice of
this canon, and hope that you might share a slice of your own. I will be
delighted to partake of the ideas that have influenced you the most!
